Sensible Suggestion.—We find the
following sensible suggestions in a
Florida paper:
Let the planter take the $150 which
he proposes to pay for an extra hand,
and invest it in manures judiciously,
and he will increase the amount of his
crop more than the work of an extra
hand. Besides he makes clear above
this the board and other extras neces-
sary t i the obtaining of hands the pres-
ent day. Then take the $200 that he
would pay for the mule, and invest in
the same way, to say nothing of the
harness and plow, and any one can
readily perceive the advantage a man
would have over his neighbor who
uses none of these fertilizers. We are
glad to see so many of our better
planters take this thing into considera-
tion, and procure large supplies of
guano and other manures. Although
the number of laborers has decreased
by hundreds in the lower counties
during the past year, yet the next crop
is anticipated to far surpass that of
1866. We trust that they will not be
disappointed.
We have given the Majority and Minon
ity Reports a careful reading. We have
earnestly endeavored to the extent of our
ability, to divest our mind of all prejudices,
, and weigh carefully the arguments con*
tained in both. We admit the truth of
the declaration, “that there is an anxious
desire on the part of the people, that there
should be a speedy restoration of constitw
tional benefits,” but we cannot believe that
I this desire outweighs all other considera-
tions.
We conceive that the people of the
South have fully discharged the obliga-
tions devolved upon them, by the unfor-
tunate issue of the war. Farther conces-
sions are out of place—ill-timed and un-
necessary. Propositions looking to out-
re-establishment in the Union, should
come from the dominant party—not us.
These propositions can be discussed, and
if unsatisfactory, can be rejected. And
here we take occasion to assert, that no
plan which invests the negro with political
privileges, will ever be entertained by the
people of Mississippi. It may be forced
unon us—carried into effect by standing
armies; but ot our own ua	—
cord, it will never be done. If our loyalty
is to be weighed by the Radical standard,
it will be found sadly deficient, and God
grant that “no change may ever come
over the spirit of our dreams” in this
respect. Insulted, belied and oppiessed ,
with the victors grasp upon us, we can
still so shape our course, as to avoid dis-
honor. “Mississippi can take no step
backward now.” Her past glories—her
future hopes—her blasted hearthstones
and desolated lands—her martyred sons
and broken promises of joy—all forbid the
act which will forever degade her. In the
future let us be found true to all obliga-
tions voluntarily assumed; but Jet us
make no puerile sacrifice of principle,
which, in years to come will prove a lasting
reproach and shame.
MORE RECONSTRUCTION FOLLY.
The new plan of reconstruction,
says the Houston Telegraph, which
some of the old chronic compromizing
political fossils are trying to hatch into
vitality, gives the right of suffrage to
every negro who can read the Consti-
tution of the United States, however
ignorant and unfitted otherwise, and
denies it to every foreign-born citizen,
French, German, Spaniard, Italian,
who cannot read English, however
highly educated and well informed. '
This negro mania is so all-absorbing
and inveterate that those who become
afflicted with it seem willing to sacri-
fice for it everything formerly deemed
true, good or honorable. And yet, if
we are to believe the telegraphic
rumors, “ leading Southern statesmen,
prominent members of Congrees, and
the President,” have agreed on it “as
a method of adjusting the political
troubles of the day.” We will not as
yet allow ourselves to believe for a
moment that either the President or
any true-hearted Southern or Con-
servative statesman has agreed to any
such compromise. The President will
not assist to deprive the States of
their constitutional right to control the
matter of suffrage within their own
limits ; and we cannot believe that any
sound Southern or Northern Conser-
vative would advise the States to vol-
untarily give up that right. Let not
the South become responsible by her
own act for any revolutionary change
which may he wrought in the govern-
ment which was framed by our
fathers.

The Radical Congress and Our Rad-
ical Legislaure.—Simultaneous with
the emphatic rejection of the President’s
plan of reconstruction by our State Senate,
comes the information from Washington,
that Mr. Stevens’ bill, (to put the South-
ern States under military rule,) has passed
the House. There can be no doubt that
the people of Mississippi ire opposed to
the new plan, because it Is an act of in-
justice. But it is equally true that what
we regard as justice will not be granted
us- We think, therefore, that those of
our legislators who, out of all the evils
threatened us, chose the least, did well.—
We envy not the course of men who, by
their votes, declare that their views must
prevail, or the greatest calamities may
come—who prefer unbridled and unlimited
negro suffrage to the State’s control on
the basis of intelligence and industry.—
But these gentlemen argue that the “North
will not accept, and why should we yield. ’ ’
We answer, if the North does not accept,
we will be no worse off by exhibiting a
conciliatory spirit; but on the contrary,
their argument that we are contumacious
and rebellious will be effectually disarmed.
Mr. Banks, a Massachusetts tyrant and
Radical,^recently declared, from his place
could be arranged by whicii''lEe"questions
could be amicably settled. The Senate
hesitates to pass the Louisiana bill of the
Riot Committee, and still no voice has
gone up from the South favoring a com-
promise to ward off the terrible evils of
military rule, the destruction of our pres-
ent State and municipal governments, and
the belching forth upon the ballot-box of
thousands of the ignorant tools of our en-
emies.

Shocking Affair, < n last Friday
night, just after dark, three men called at
the house of our friend Milton Bene, 4
miles from Gallatin, on the road to Natch-
ez, and enquired if two travelers had
stopped there. On being told that they
had not, one of them advanced into the
house, leaving the other two at the door,
and ordered Mr. Bene into his bed room,
drawing at the same time a six-shooter.
Mrs. Bene rushed from her room and
placed herself between the ruffian and her
husband, the robber trying all the time to
shoot Mr. Bene. A little son of Mr. Bene,
about ten years old, sprang from his bed,
seized a double-barrelled shot-gun and shot
the robber down. The robber, on purpose
or by accident, knocked the candle out
and left them in the dark. Mr. Bene,
thinking that the robber had fired his
pistol, rushed from the house for assis-
tance, . when one of the two at the door
came into the room, and found that his
comrade was wounded, and, perhaps,
killed. They both left hastily, conveying
led horse. The wounded man recovering
somewhat, scrambled to the door and fell
down the Steps, helloing for his comrades
who returned and earned him off. The
robber left his pistol, saddle-bags and hat.
Up to this . time nothing is known of the
men on their object. The man who at-
tacked Mr. Bene was tall, of sandy com-
plexion and red whiskers. The commun-
ity ought to be on the look out for the
scoundrels. In one end of the saddle-
bags was found two pieces of candles
wrapped up in one of Finney K Go’s cir-
culars, harness makers, Vicksburg.

The River Convention.—The
Louis Democrat, of the 4th, says of
this great movement:
“ With commendable energy the
delegates to the River Improvement
Convention have dispatched their busi-
ness, and with two days’ sessions have
adjourned' sine die. Much as this
great subject has been agitated by the
farmers and business men of the Val-
ley of the Mississippi, and ventilated
through the columns of newspapers
for nearly thirty years, the Convention
has absolutely effected the only practi-
cal result yet attained. It has not, it is
true, accomplished the desired object,
but in the resolutions offered and
unanimously adopted, it has given a
direction and impetus to legislation,
which, if properly followed up, can
scarcely fail of ultimate success. No
body of men ever convened for any
given purpose, where the singleness of
purpose which inspired such harmony
in the Convention can compel proper
action on the part of the General Gov-
ernment, and thus effect the greatest
possible service for the^ people of this

Sinking of the White Cloud.—We
condense from the St. Louis Times an ac-
count of the sinking of the White Cloud
No. 2, which will, we trust, prove of iir
derest to our readers:
"The White Cloud No. 2, in command
of Captain Conley, left this port for New
Orleans, at about half-past seven o’clock
on Wednesday night. In less than twen-
ty minutes after getting under way, and
while yet within sight of the city, shs ran
on to the wreck of the old steamer Belle
Memphis, and sunk. Mr. Gus. Shankle
was the pilot on watch at the time, and
his partner, Captain Barney Begley was
in the wheel house. Capt. Conley and the
mate were on the roof. No lives were
lost. The boat only sunk to the boiler
deck, and was nearly twenty minutes in
sinking. All the crew and passengers got
on the hurricane roof and were taken to
shore by the yawls of the steamers Benton
and Kate Hart, which were sent to thei r
assistance. The boat lodged on the "break”
just in front of her boilers, with her bow
and stern drooping. At three o’clock in
the morning of yesterday she broke in two,
and will prove a total loss. The steamer
Minor was lying beside her yesterday, en-
deavoring to save some of her furniture
and freight. It is not probable that much
can be saved.”
There were on board ten ladies who be-
haved with great heroism and coolness, in-
spiring the "sterner sex” by their exam1
pie.
A cabin boy who was on his first trip,
in fright, jumped overboard, and seizing
the tail of a cow near by, was towed safely
to shore.
We understand that the White Cloud
was owned by W. M. McPherson, Esq.,
of this city. Her valuation by the Board
of Underwriters was $55,000. She was
an excellent boat, only about two years
old, and was doubtless worth $65,000 or
$70,000. She was insured for $40,000
Of this insurance $30,000 were in Cincin-
nati offices, and $10,000 were in Eastern
companies. One policy of $5,000 was in
the New England Mutual of Boston. Her
cargo was probably worth $250,000 or
$300,000) and was insured in St. Louis
offices.
